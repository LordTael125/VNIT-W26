Story: We Bought a Shuttle—Did We Create a Transport System?
Imagine that VNIT decides to introduce an electric
shuttle service on campus.

Three years later, the battery needs
replacement—but the supplier has
discontinued that model.
Someone asks: ‘We understood the
system before introducing it. Why are
we facing these problems?’
The transport coordinator replies: ‘We
understood how its parts interact. But
did we design how the service would be
introduced, operated, maintained,
adapted and eventually replaced?’

We begin with systems thinking. We examine
students, drivers, vehicles, roads, charging stations,
timetables and pedestrians. We explore their
interactions: a delayed shuttle creates a longer queue;
a longer queue increases boarding time; longer
boarding time causes further delay. We also ask
where to draw the boundary—should our analysis
include electricity supply, rainy weather and
examination schedules?
Having understood these relationships, we purchase a
shuttle. On its rst day, the service works well.

“Systems thinking helped us see the shuttle service as a whole. Lifecycle engineering extends that view across time—so that decisions
made today support the system’s purpose throughout its life.”

Story: From Forest to City
Imagine a forest. It is full of natural
systems — trees growing, rivers owing,
animals surviving. Each of these came into
being through evolution, not human
design. Functions came rst:
• The heart pumps blood,
• The eye senses light,
• The leaf converts sunlight to energy.
Structures changed across species, but
functions remained constant.

• Power system → provide reliable energy.
• Hospital IT → give doctors secure access to
patient data.
In the forest, systems are shaped by natural
selection.
In the city, systems are shaped by systems
engineering.

And so, as engineers, when we move from analyzing “what systems
are” (state, situation, behavior, process, function) → to “how we bring
them into being,” we are stepping into the same role nature plays in
evolution — but with foresight, creativity, and responsibility.
“When we design systems, we are doing consciously what nature has
always done unconsciously — shaping functions into structures,
creating order in the human-made world.”

The difference?
• Nature “designed” for survival.
• Humans design for purpose, ef ciency, and resilience.
