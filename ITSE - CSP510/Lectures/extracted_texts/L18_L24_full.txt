Story: We Bought a Shuttle—Did We Create a Transport System?
Imagine that VNIT decides to introduce an electric 
shuttle service on campus.
We begin with systems thinking. We examine 
students, drivers, vehicles, roads, charging stations, 
timetables and pedestrians. We explore their 
interactions: a delayed shuttle creates a longer queue; 
a longer queue increases boarding time; longer 
boarding time causes further delay. We also ask 
where to draw the boundary—should our analysis 
include electricity supply, rainy weather and 
examination schedules?
Having understood these relationships, we purchase a 
shuttle. On its ﬁrst day, the service works well.
Six months later, its range has reduced. Charging 
overlaps with peak travel hours. A software update 
changes the estimated arrival times. The trained 
driver leaves, and a replacement needs instruction. 
Students now attend classes in a new building, 
beyond the original route.
Three years later, the battery needs 
replacement—but the supplier has 
discontinued that model.
Someone asks: ‘We understood the 
system before introducing it. Why are 
we facing these problems?’
The transport coordinator replies: ‘We 
understood how its parts interact. But 
did we design how the service would be 
introduced, operated, maintained, 
adapted and eventually replaced?’
3
Which of these later problems should have inﬂuenced our initial design decisions?
“Systems thinking helped us see the shuttle service as a whole. Life-
cycle engineering extends that view across time—so that decisions 
made today support the system’s purpose throughout its life.”

From understanding the system to engineering its life
•The shuttle story raises a basic question: If we understood the system before 
introducing it, why did problems appear later?
•Our initial understanding may have captured the interactions operating at that time. 
However, the system and its environment continue to change. 
•Batteries age, people leave, demand shifts, software changes and suppliers 
discontinue components. Engineering decisions must therefore consider how the 
system will fulﬁl its purpose under these changing conditions.
•Systems thinking already encourages us to examine change over time.
• Life-cycle engineering turns that understanding into requirements, design 
choices, supporting capabilities and decisions throughout the system’s 
existence.
5
What are we bringing into existence?
In the shuttle example, the intended outcome is 
reliable and accessible campus mobility. Purchasing 
an electric vehicle contributes to this outcome, but 
several other capabilities are necessary:
•routes and schedules that match travel needs;
•charging facilities and electricity availability;
•trained drivers and operating procedures;
•maintenance, spare parts and failure recovery;
•passenger information and feedback;
•arrangements for upgrades, replacement and 
retirement.
•The vehicle is a product within the 
campus mobility system. Its usefulness 
depends on these relationships.
•To realize a system is to bring its 
intended capability into operation 
through interacting technical, 
human and organizational elements.
•This also explains why systems 
engineering applies to services. 
•A healthcare service, university 
registration service or cloud computing 
service must be developed, operated, 
supported, changed and eventually 
replaced or withdrawn.6
What does the life-cycle perspective add?
•A life-cycle perspective connects present decisions with future 
consequences.
•For example, choosing a shuttle battery determines more than its 
initial range. It also affects charging time, route scheduling, 
replacement cost, maintenance skills, supplier dependence and 
disposal arrangements.
•We must therefore ask:
•What does this decision enable, constrain or make necessary later 
in the system’s life?
•This question applies to architecture, technology, stafﬁng, contracts, 
data, software and operating procedures.

A problem discovered in operation may require reconsidering a design choice—or even the original concept. The life 
cycle therefore involves both progression and feedback.9
Future stages inﬂuence present design
Maintenance begins operationally after deployment, but maintainability must 
inﬂuence design before deployment. The same principle applies to training, recovery, 
upgrading and retirement.
For the shuttle:
•Future battery replacement inﬂuences the initial battery and supplier choice.
•Driver turnover inﬂuences training materials and procedure design.
•Route expansion inﬂuences capacity and charging provision.
•Software failure inﬂuences manual operating arrangements.
•Retirement inﬂuences material recovery and replacement planning.
This is the practical meaning of designing for the whole life cycle.
A later activity often requires an earlier design provision.

Engineering the enabling systems
•The shuttle service has its own life cycle, but so do the capabilities that enable it.
•Charging infrastructure must be designed, installed, maintained and renewed. 
•Maintenance capability requires tools, facilities, staff and spare-parts 
arrangements. 
•Training must be developed and updated as vehicles and procedures change.
•These life cycles are related. Their readiness and changes must be coordinated. 
•Delivering the vehicle before charging, training or support is ready does not 
deliver an operational transport capability.

Feedback sustains the connection between purpose and reality
- Once the service operates, engineers can compare expected behaviour with observed behaviour.
- Suppose waiting times increase. The cause might be insufﬁcient capacity, delayed charging, longer 
boarding times or a mismatch between schedules and demand. Each explanation suggests a different 
intervention.
Effective feedback requires:
•suitable observations;
•interpretation in the operating context;
•a person or team authorized to act;
•a way to evaluate whether the change improved the outcome.
- Feedback may lead to an operating adjustment, maintenance action, design modiﬁcation or 
reconsideration of requirements.

Story: From Forest to City
Imagine a forest. It is full of natural 
systems — trees growing, rivers ﬂowing, 
animals surviving. Each of these came into 
being through evolution, not human 
design. Functions came ﬁrst:
•The heart pumps blood,
•The eye senses light,
•The leaf converts sunlight to energy. 
Structures changed across species, but 
functions remained constant.
Now shift to a city nearby. This is the human-
made world. The city has its own systems: 
water supply, electricity, transport, hospitals, 
digital networks. None of these systems evolved 
by chance. They had to be designed. But here 
too, functions come ﬁrst:
•Water system → deliver clean water.
•Power system → provide reliable energy.
•Hospital IT → give doctors secure access to 
patient data.
In the forest, systems are shaped by natural 
selection. 
In the city, systems are shaped by systems 
engineering.
The difference?
•Nature “designed” for survival.
•Humans design for purpose, efﬁciency, and resilience.17
And so, as engineers, when we move from analyzing “what systems 
are” (state, situation, behavior, process, function) → to “how we bring 
them into being,” we are stepping into the same role nature plays in 
evolution — but with foresight, creativity, and responsibility.
“When we design systems, we are doing consciously what nature has 
always done unconsciously — shaping functions into structures, 
creating order in the human-made world.”
18
Natural systems develop through evolutionary and ecological processes. Structures, functions and 
interactions change together, without a predeﬁned goal.
In engineered systems, identiﬁed needs and intended functions guide design. Engineers develop and 
reﬁne structures using analysis, testing and feedback.
Systems thinking helps us understand the whole system; life-cycle engineering applies that 
understanding from conception through operation, renewal and retirement.

The Engineered System
Deﬁnition
•An engineered (technical) system = a human-made or human-modiﬁed system 
designed to meet functional purposes or objectives.
•In this course, “engineered system” implies a well-engineered system — 
purposeful, effective, and sustainable.
• But we must understand that a human-designed system can be ineffective, unsafe 
or unsustainable.
• We should evaluate engineering quality rather than assume it from the deﬁnition.
21
Characteristics of a Well-Engineered System
1.Functional Purpose
◦Built in response to identiﬁed needs.
◦Must achieve stated operational objectives.
2.Life Cycle Existence
◦From needs identiﬁcation → design → 
production & deployment → operation → 
phase-out & disposal.
3.Design Momentum
◦Increases through design, production, and 
deployment.
◦Decreases through phase-out, retirement, and 
disposal.
4.Resource Integration
◦Combines facilities, equipment, materials, 
people, information, software, and money into 
a harmonized whole.
5.Subsystems & Components
◦Built from interacting subsystems/components 
that together create the system’s response or 
behavior.
6.Hierarchy & Inﬂuence
◦Each system is part of a larger system-of-
systems.
◦Inﬂuenced by external factors and sibling 
systems.
7.Embedded in the Natural World
◦Always interacts with the natural environment 
— sometimes positively, sometimes negatively.
22
Systems Engineering as a Discipline
•Deﬁnition: A functionally oriented, technologically based, 
interdisciplinary process for:
◦Bringing new human-made systems into being.
◦Improving legacy systems.
•Overarching Purpose: Make the world better for people by:
◦Satisfying human needs effectively.
◦Minimizing life-cycle costs.
◦Reducing ecological and societal impacts.
23
Organizational Strategy
•Organization = humanity’s greatest innovation for creating systems.
•Focus not on entities themselves, but on what entities are intended to do.
•In architecture: “Form follows function.”
•In Systems Engineering: Focus on designing, delivering, and sustaining:
◦Functionality
◦Capability
◦Solution

An engineered system is not just hardware or software — it is a purposeful, 
organized, life-cycle entity, designed with functions ﬁrst, then form. Systems 
engineering ensures these systems are brought into being efﬁciently, sustainably, and 
with human needs at the center. 24

Two Generic Categories of Engineered Systems
A. Single-Entity Product Systems
•Examples:
◦Bridge, custom-designed software, home entertainment center.
◦Custom service → counseling session, banquet.
•Classiﬁcation:
◦Consumables (e.g., banquet, counseling session).
◦Repairables (e.g., bridge, highway, supercomputer).
◦Consumer goods vs Producer goods (the latter produce the former).
•Key Insight:
◦The product alone ≠ engineered system.
◦Engineered system = product + construction subsystem + maintenance subsystem + 
operating & support subsystem + phase-out subsystem.
◦Example: A bridge → not just the structure, but also design, maintenance, operations, and 
eventual demolition.
•Other single-entity systems: manufacturing plants, warehouses, hospitals, air trafﬁc control 
centers. 27
B. Multiple-Entity Population Systems
•Deﬁned: Populations of homogeneous or near-homogeneous entities, studied probabilistically.
•Examples:
◦Airlines or military aircraft ﬂeets.
◦Ground transit → taxis, rental cars, commercial trucks.
◦Production equipment → looms, autoclaves, machine tools.
◦Housing complexes (multi-family units).
◦Ofﬁce buildings (multi-tenant).
◦Public clinics in urban/suburban areas.
◦Inventories → consumables (batteries, food, toiletries) and repairables (aircraft pumps, automobile starters, 
automation controllers).
•Characteristics:
◦Populations allow economies of scale in design/production.
◦Enable maintenance learning and statistical analysis (mortality, failure rates).
◦Still, the engineered system > population itself, since support, regeneration, and phase-out subsystems are 
essential.

Example: Smartphone vs Metro Rail System
Smartphone → Product Life Cycle
•Need: Portable personal communication + computing.
•Conceptual/Preliminary Design: Decide size, features (touchscreen, battery, 
connectivity).
•Detail Design & Development: Chipset design, OS, UI, apps.
•Production/Construction: Mass manufacturing in factories, assembly lines.
•Utilization Phase: Customer purchase, use, updates, repairs.
•Phase-out & Disposal: E-waste recycling, resale markets, replacement.

•Factories exist, but are external to the user’s view.
•Support (warranty, repair shops) is important but secondary to the product.
