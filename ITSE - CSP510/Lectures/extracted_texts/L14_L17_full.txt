Story: The Broken Clock Tower
In a busy town square stood a giant clock tower, built more than 100 years ago. One day, the clock 
stopped working.
The mayor called in three experts:
•A mechanical engineer looked at the gears and said: “The cogs are worn out. Replace them and it 
will work again.”
•An electrical engineer checked the wiring and declared: “The circuits are faulty. Fix them and 
the clock will tick.”
•A software engineer examined the new digital display and concluded: “The program is outdated. 
Upgrade it, and time will be correct again.”
Each expert was right — but even after ﬁxing gears, wires, and software, the clock still wouldn’t run 
properly.
Finally, an old watchmaker came along. He didn’t just look at parts; he looked at how the whole 
system — gears, wiring, display, weather exposure, and even how townsfolk maintained it — 
interacted. He adjusted not just one element, but how they all worked together.
Within hours, the clock was running perfectly again.
Moral for Students
•Each discipline sees a part of the system.
•But only when we think like the watchmaker — in terms of interactions, feedback, and 
the whole — do we get to real solutions.
•This is what we mean by Systems Thinking: not ﬁxing parts in isolation, but 
understanding how the whole system behaves.
How do we relate this to Systems Engineering: 
Just like the clock tower, every modern engineering system — from hospitals to metros to 
smart grids — needs us to think in terms of systems, not parts. 
We’ll explore how engineers across disciplines see systems, and how this evolves from 
multidisciplinary to transdisciplinary thinking.
But there is another dimension to the story: 
What if the clock could now observe its own performance, learn from its 
environment, change how it operates, and call other software tools to 
correct itself?
Is our original system boundary still adequate?
traditional system → software-intensive system → AI-enabled adaptive/agentic system.

Introduction
1. What is Systems Thinking?
•A holistic approach to understanding complex entities not just as the 
sum of parts but as interdependent wholes.
•Emphasizes relationships, interconnections, feedback, and emergence.
•Opposed to reductionist thinking, which isolates components without 
capturing interactions.
•Useful for problems where linear cause-effect is insufﬁcient.
Key Principles:
1.Interconnectedness – Every element inﬂuences and is inﬂuenced 
by others.
2.Feedback Loops – Positive (reinforcing) and negative 
(balancing) loops govern system behavior.
3.Emergence – System-level properties not found in individual 
components.
4.Boundaries and Perspectives – The way we draw system 
boundaries changes how we see the problem.
5.Leverage Points – Small changes in system structure can create 
large-scale impact.
Systems Thinking vs. Engineering Viewpoints
•Systems Thinking is a mindset: seeing the “whole” before the “parts.”
•Engineering Viewpoints are structured frameworks or lenses through which engineers analyze 
systems.
Engineering Viewpoints include:
1.Functional viewpoint – What the system must do.
2.Structural viewpoint – How the system is organized (architecture, modules, components).
3.Behavioral viewpoint – How the system responds dynamically to inputs and environments.
4.Physical viewpoint – Real-world instantiation (hardware, networks, physical processes).
5.Lifecycle viewpoint – From conception to decommissioning.
6.Stakeholder viewpoint – Different stakeholders perceive systems differently (operator, user, 
regulator).
Together, they prevent engineers from “blind spots” by ensuring multiple perspectives.

Why Systems Thinking Matters for Engineers
•Complex socio-technical problems (autonomous vehicles, 
smart grids, hospitals, defense systems) cannot be solved 
by one discipline.
•Helps engineers avoid local optimization (subsystem 
perfection but system failure).
•Supports design for resilience, robustness, and 
adaptability.
Pedagogical Example
•Case: Metro rail system.
◦Systems thinking: Focus on how passengers, operators, ticketing, 
trafﬁc, and urban development interact.
◦Engineering viewpoints: Break into signaling system (functional), 
rolling stock design (physical), scheduling (behavioral), lifecycle 
maintenance (lifecycle), passenger experience (stakeholder).

We looked at the Metro Rail System as an example of a modern, complex 
system. We saw how many subsystems come together — civil engineering 
for tracks, electrical for traction, electronics for signaling, and computer 
science for scheduling.
But if we stop at only naming subsystems, we are still thinking in silos.
To go deeper, we need a way of thinking that tells us:
•How do these subsystems interact?
•What patterns repeat across very different systems?
•Why do some designs succeed while others fail, even if the parts 
are well-made?
This way of thinking was ﬁrst systematically described by Ludwig von 
Bertalanffy in his classic work General System Theory more than 50 years 
ago. His insights gave birth to what we now call Systems Thinking.
And what’s fascinating is — even though he wrote in the 1960s, the 
principles are still valid today in the AI-driven world we live in. For 
example:
•His idea of open systems explains why AI models must continuously 
interact with new data.
•His principle of feedback loops is exactly how neural networks learn.
•His concept of emergence helps us understand why whole systems (like 
smart cities or healthcare networks) behave in ways that no single 
subsystem can explain.
So now, let’s step back from the metro rails, and see what 
Bertalanffy’s fundamentals of Systems Thinking can 
teach us — principles that apply not just to metros, but to 
every system we will ever design or interact with.”

Systems Engineering Perspective
In Systems Engineering, mathematics is not just calculation — it is structure.
•Systems engineers look for patterns of interconnection, ﬂow, control, and 
optimization that repeat in physical, digital, and social systems.
•Graph theory and optimization are two of the most universal tools because they 
model:
◦Relationships (who connects to whom, what depends on what), and
◦Decisions (how to allocate, minimize, maximize, or balance).
Thus, both AI systems and logistics systems can be understood as networks of 
interacting elements seeking optimal states.

Systems-Theoretic Implication: Same Math, Different Systems
In Systems Engineering, this is viewed as abstraction equivalence:
•Different systems (AI, logistics, networks, ecosystems) exhibit the same structure 
of feedback, ﬂow, and optimization.
•The mathematics gives us a universal language to:
◦Model interdependencies (graphs),
◦Quantify performance (objective functions), and
◦Search for optimal or resilient conﬁgurations (optimization algorithms).
This is what Bertalanffy meant by isomorphism across systems:
“Similar structures of relations appear in different ﬁelds — physics, biology, sociology 
— because the pattern of interdependence is the same.”
Real-World Example
AI-driven logistics optimization (Amazon / FedEx / Swiggy):
•Graph theory: models delivery network and dependencies.
•AI (Reinforcement Learning): learns optimal routing decisions from experience.
•Optimization algorithms: minimize total delivery cost and time under 
constraints.
So — AI learns the rules dynamically, logistics provides the physical 
constraints, and systems engineering integrates both into a functioning socio-
technical system.
“Whether you are training a neural network or routing trucks across India, you are 
solving the same mathematical problem — optimizing ﬂows and decisions on a graph. 
Systems Engineering teaches you to recognize the same structure beneath different 
technologies.”

Systems Thinking Fundamentals from Checkland (Reframed for Today)
Systemic vs. Systematic Thinking
•Systematic = linear, step-by-step, reductionist, recipe-driven.
◦Good for well-bounded, clear problems (e.g., software 
debugging).
•Systemic = holistic, contextual, relational, emergent.
◦Needed for messy, complex, socio-technical problems (e.g., 
regulating AI bias, urban transport planning).
•Today: Engineers must combine both. Example: Training an AI 
model (systematic) + deploying it responsibly in society (systemic).
Boundaries and Perspectives
•Systems are not “found” but formulated by drawing boundaries.
•Different stakeholders deﬁne boundaries differently, depending on 
perspectives.
•Today: In AI ethics, regulators, developers, and users draw boundaries 
differently (privacy vs. utility vs. proﬁt). Recognizing multiple perspectives is 
essential.
Connectivity and Feedback
•A system is deﬁned by interconnections and ﬂows 
(information, energy, money).
•Feedback loops (positive, negative) drive stability or 
chaos.
•Today: AI recommendation systems reinforce biases 
(positive feedback) or regulate content (negative 
feedback). Understanding feedback helps prevent 
unintended consequences.

--- PAGE 12 ---
Emergence and Unintended Consequences
•Whole systems display properties not present in parts 
(emergence).
•Poorly designed systems often produce unintended 
consequences (e.g., trafﬁc jams after building new 
highways).
•Today: Generative AI produces creativity (emergence) but 
also misinformation (unintended consequence).
Hard vs. Soft Systems Thinking
•Hard systems tradition: World contains systems that can be 
engineered; problem-solution orientation.
•Soft systems tradition: World is problematical; systems models 
are constructs to explore perspectives and enable learning.
•Today:
◦Hard systems → algorithm development, robotic design.
◦Soft systems → stakeholder negotiation in AI ethics, healthcare 
IT adoption.
◦Both needed together.
Messy (Wicked) Problems
•Checkland emphasized “messes” (Ackoff) and “wicked 
problems” (Rittel & Webber).
•Wicked problems have no single deﬁnition or solution — 
multiple stakeholders, shifting goals.
•Today: Regulating autonomous weapons, ensuring fairness 
in AI, climate change adaptation.
Purposeful vs. Purposive Systems
•Purposive: Purpose is imposed externally (e.g., a thermostat).
•Purposeful: Systems can deﬁne and adapt their own purpose (e.g., 
human organizations, AI agents with reinforcement learning).
•Today: AI agents and autonomous vehicles increasingly show 
purposeful behavior, requiring ethical oversight.

Tools and Practices
Checkland emphasized practical methods for working 
systemically. Still highly relevant:
•Rich Pictures → unstructured sketches to capture messy 
realities (great for AI project scoping).
•Systems Maps → show elements, boundaries, environment.
•Multiple Cause Diagrams → show feedback and causality.
•Inﬂuence Diagrams → map inﬂuence pathways.
•Control Models → explore how systems maintain balance.
These remain useful lab tools: hand-drawn, conceptual, 
resistant to “copy-paste” shortcuts.
Relevance for Today’s AI-driven World
1.Systemic vs. systematic → AI projects must balance coding rigor with 
ethical/social considerations.
2.Boundaries → In AI, who deﬁnes “system of interest” matters 
(developers, government, citizens).
3.Emergence → AI systems display emergent creativity but also 
emergent risks.
4.Wicked Problems → No simple solutions; e.g., balancing innovation 
with fairness in AI.
5.Tools → Rich pictures, causal maps, and inﬂuence diagrams are still 
among the best ways to understand AI-society interactions.

What AI Changes — and What Systems Thinking Does Not Let Us Forget
Transition from Bertalanffy → Checkland:
•Bertalanffy gave us the scientiﬁc foundation: systems are open, 
hierarchical, emergent. Checkland built on that, showing us how to 
practice systems thinking in messy, human situations. 
•In today’ s AI-driven world, we need both — the scientiﬁc lens of 
Bertalanffy and the practical, human-centered lens of Checkland.

“messy” contexts.
So: Bertalanffy = “What is a system?” 
Checkland = “How do we work with systems in practice?”
