CSL510 ISE W26 The Orchestra That Could Not Make Music
Imagine an orchestra assembled with the ﬁnest musicians. The violinist is excellent. The pianist is 
excellent. The percussionist never misses a beat. Every instrument is perfectly tuned.
The conductor asks each musician to demonstrate individually. Everyone performs ﬂawlessly.
Then the entire orchestra begins.
It sounds terrible.
Nothing is wrong with any musician. Nothing is wrong with any instrument.
The problem is between them—their timing, coordination, listening, response to one another, and 
interpretation of the conductor.
Where does the music reside—in the musicians, in the instruments, or somewhere else?
This will set the premise of today’s lecture:
The musicians are the components. 
Their coordination creates relationships. 
Playing together creates interactions. 
The score and conductor organize those interactions. 
And music is an emergent property of the whole.
“If I replace every musician with a better musician, will I necessarily get better music?”
Not necessarily.
This single question captures a central Systems Engineering lesson: optimizing components does not 
necessarily optimize the system.

Why is a collection of components not necessarily a system?
Recap of the three conditions:
•Each component affects the whole.
•Each component depends on at least one other component.
•The components cannot be partitioned into completely independent subsets.
What makes these components behave as a whole?
This becomes the central question of the lecture.
Components and Relationships
A component is an identiﬁable part of the system, but 
merely listing components tells us very little about the 
system.
 Different relationships:
•Physical — road–vehicle, battery–charger
•Information — sensor–controller
•Control — controller–actuator
•Energy — grid–charging station
•Material — reservoir–pipeline
•Human–technical — driver–vehicle
•Organizational — department–central administration
•Economic — service–payment/budget
Analytical Window
Two systems contain exactly 
the same components. 
Must they necessarily be 
the same system?
No. Different relationships 
among identical components 
can produce entirely different 
systems.
This is an important systems-
thinking insight.
Relationship is Not the Same as Interaction
Let us make this distinction explicitly.
Relationship: A is connected to B.
Interaction: What happens between A and B?
For example:
Student ↔ Campus transport system
The relationship exists because students use the transport service.
Interactions include:
•student requests transport;
•system receives location/demand;
•system allocates vehicle;
•vehicle transports student;
•system records journey.
Therefore:
Relationships describe connectivity; interactions produce system behaviour.
What Flows Through an Interaction?
We need to look beyond data ﬂow.
Across system components may ﬂow:
•Information
•Control
•Energy
•Material
•Money
•People
•Services
•Responsibility/authority
Example: Smart irrigation
Soil sensor → data → controller 
Controller → control command → valve 
Water source → water → ﬁeld 
Grid/solar → energy → pump 
Farmer → decision/override → system
A software engineer therefore needs to 
understand not merely the information 
ﬂow but the physical consequence of 
information and control.

Interfaces — Where Interactions Become 
Engineering
Let us  Introduce interface:
If two components must interact, how is that 
interaction actually realized?
An interface speciﬁes the point and conditions 
of interaction.
Software view: API, protocol, message format, 
database interface.
Systems view: much broader:
•software–software;
•hardware–software;
•sensor–physical environment;
•human–machine;
•electrical;
•mechanical;
•organizational;
•system–external environment.
Example: E-vehicle charging
The interface is not merely the 
charging connector.
It involves:
Vehicle ↔ charger ↔ power grid 
↔ payment/authorization ↔ 
scheduling software ↔ user
V oltage compatibility may be correct 
while authentication fails. 
Software may work while power is 
unavailable.
Hence:
Interface correctness is not merely 
technical compatibility; it is 
successful interaction in the 
operational situation.
Dependency
What happens to B when A changes?
Examples:
•Charging station unavailable → vehicle availability falls.
•GPS gives incorrect location → scheduling decisions deteriorate.
•ERP fails → warehouse may physically contain material but procurement 
transactions stop.
•Power failure → water pumps stop → water distribution changes.
This helps us establish dependency.
Coupling
Dependencies differ in strength.
Loosely coupled
A change in one component has limited 
consequences elsewhere.
Tightly coupled
Changes propagate quickly or strongly 
across components.
Example:
A classroom projector failure is 
relatively localized.
Failure of the campus authentication 
service could simultaneously affect Wi-
Fi, ERP access, library systems and 
other services.
Is tighter coupling always bad?
No. Tight coupling may provide 
efﬁciency, speed and coordination, but 
can also increase propagation risk.
This prepares us for trade-offs and 
resilience.
From Local Behaviour to System Behaviour
Let us now make a distinction that we often miss.
A component has its own behaviour:
•bus moves;
•sensor measures;
•algorithm predicts;
•charger supplies energy.
But the system behaviour results from their interactions.
For campus transportation:
Vehicle movement + student demand + schedules + charging + road 
conditions + dispatch decisions 
→ transport-system behaviour
Therefore, studying each component individually may still fail to predict 
what the whole system will do.

Where is “campus mobility” located?
Not in the bus. 
Not in the driver. 
Not in the scheduling algorithm. 
Not in the charging station.
Yet the complete system provides mobility.

A system can exhibit multiple emergent properties, and which ones become visible
—and to what degree—depends on the interactions, operating situation, boundary, 
scale, time, and observer's measure of interest.

How Do We “See” Emergence?
If emergence belongs to the whole system, where should we look for it?
Not inside an individual component.
Look instead at:
Components → Relationships → Interactions → Collective Behaviour → System-level 
Property
Take your campus e-transport example:
•Vehicle → moves people
•Charger → transfers energy
•Scheduling system → allocates vehicles
•Students → generate demand
•Roads → provide paths
•Drivers → operate vehicles
None individually possesses campus mobility.
But their coordinated interaction produces:
Mobility as an emergent property
So, a practical way of identifying emergence is:
Ask: “What can the whole system do, exhibit, or experience that cannot meaningfully be 
attributed to any single component?”
But There Is Not Just One 
Emergence
We will have conceptual 
advancement:  The same campus 
transportation system may 
simultaneously exhibit:
Desirable emergence
•Accessibility
•Mobility
•Energy efﬁciency
•Convenience
•Adaptability
•Resilience
Undesirable emergence
•Congestion
•Long queues
•Unequal service
•Charging bottlenecks
•Cascading delays
•Unsafe driving patterns
Therefore:
Emergence is not synonymous with “beneﬁt.”
Nor is it necessarily something deliberately designed.
Some emergent properties are intended, some 
unintended, and some may only become apparent 
during operation.
What Determines Which Emergence 
Appears?
Consider an emergent property 
conceptually as dependent on:
Components + interaction structure + 
operating situation + time + system 
boundary
1. Component properties
Vehicle capacity, battery capacity, charging 
rate, sensor accuracy.
2. Interaction structure
Who communicates with whom? 
Which vehicle uses which charger? 
How are routes coordinated?
3. Interaction strength/frequency
How frequently are vehicles dispatched? 
How strongly does demand inﬂuence 
scheduling?
4. Operating situation
Normal class day, examination day, convocation, 
heavy rain, vehicle breakdown.
5. Feedback
Does the system detect queues and modify dispatch? 
Does it learn from previous demand?
6. Time
A conﬁguration that appears efﬁcient for 30 minutes 
may create charging congestion three hours later.
7. Boundary
If you consider only vehicles, you may see ﬂeet 
efﬁciency. 
Include yourself (students) and you see accessibility. 
Include the power system and you may see energy 
sustainability.
This last point is particularly valuable for Systems 
Engineering.

Emergence Is Observer- and Level-
Dependent
This deserves attention because it 
develops systems thinking.
Imagine the same campus 
transportation system.
A student observes:
“Can I get to my class on time?”
Emergence of interest → accessibility
The Estate/Transport Section 
observes:
“How effectively is the ﬂeet being 
utilized?”
Emergence of interest → operational 
efﬁciency
Institute management observes:
“Has campus dependence on private vehicles reduced?”
Emergence of interest → sustainable mobility
Therefore, don't say that emergence 
itself is purely subjective. Rather:
The system may possess several 
system-level properties; what becomes 
important or observable depends 
partly on the system boundary, level 
of abstraction, stakeholder and 
measure of interest.
This distinction is important.
How Can an Engineer Analyze Emergence?
A usable procedure.
Emergence Analysis Window
For any system:
1.Deﬁne the system boundary.
2.Identify important components.
3.Map signiﬁcant relationships and interfaces.
4.Identify what ﬂows across them — information, control, energy, material, 
people, resources.
5.Vary the situation or operating condition.
6.Observe the resulting collective behaviour.
7.Identify properties visible only at the system level.
8.Classify them: desirable/undesirable; intended/unintended.
9.Identify which interactions amplify or suppress each property.
10.Ask what happens if a component, relationship or feedback rule changes.
This transforms emergence from an abstract philosophical concept into something 
you can actually analyze during system design.
Example for CSE Students:
Distributed trafﬁc-signal control
Suppose every junction has an AI controller.
Each controller has one objective:
Minimize waiting time at its own junction.
Every AI may perform its assigned function 
correctly.
Yet at the city level, their independent 
decisions can potentially produce:
•trafﬁc waves,
•downstream congestion,
•starvation of minor roads,
•unstable switching patterns,
•or network-wide gridlock.
Now change the interactions. Allow 
controllers to exchange queue and 
downstream-capacity information.
Potentially different properties emerge:
•coordinated ﬂow,
•adaptive routing,
•congestion containment,
•faster recovery after incidents.
The important Systems Engineering 
lesson is:
Emergence is produced not merely 
by intelligent components, but by the 
organization of their interactions.
This is highly relevant to contemporary 
AI engineering.
Emergence Can Compete With Emergence
Improving one emergent property can 
degrade another:
Maximum vehicle utilization 
→ fewer idle vehicles 
→ improved efﬁciency
but possibly
→ reduced spare capacity 
→ poorer resilience during sudden demand.
Similarly:
Aggressive trafﬁc-ﬂow 
optimization 
→ reduced average travel time
but potentially
→ poorer pedestrian accessibility 
or safety.
Thus system design is not simply:
“Create emergence.”
It is:
Understand, shape and balance 
multiple emergent properties 
under changing situations and 
constraints.

Place of AI in this Picture
Now let us make the lecture contemporary.
AI can become:
A component
An AI model performs prediction/
classiﬁcation.
An interaction mediator
AI determines how other components 
interact—vehicle dispatch, energy 
allocation, trafﬁc control.
A feedback mechanism
Observe → analyze → decide → act → 
observe again.
But the systems question remains vital:
If an AI component locally optimizes its 
objective, does it necessarily improve the 
whole system?
Not necessarily.
For example, an AI trafﬁc controller may 
maximize ﬂow on one road while creating 
congestion elsewhere.
Therefore:
Local intelligence ≠ System intelligence
This we need to understand  as we are coming 
from a Computer Science’s perspective but are 
now learning Systems Engineering.

Closing Analytical Window
Let us ﬁnish with three questions:
If every component of a system is 
working correctly, can the system still 
fail?
Can improving one component make the 
overall system worse?
Can a property exist at the system level 
that exists in none of its components?
If we can reason through these three questions, 
we have moved from a component-oriented 
engineering mindset toward systems thinking.
So far we have looked inside the system.
The next question then is: 
“But where does the system end?”
This gives rise to three more 
questions: 
1. Can the same components 
produce different emergent 
properties? What must change?
2. Can improving the behaviour 
of every component individually 
worsen a system-level property?
3. If AI continuously changes 
interactions within a system, are 
the emergent properties 
themselves likely to remain 
static?
