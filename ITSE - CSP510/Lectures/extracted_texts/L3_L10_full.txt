Anecdote: The Train That Never Stopped at Night
Context:
This occurred in aEuropean city’s newly automated
metro system. The system was highly advanced —
featuring real-time scheduling, AI-driven energy
optimization, and automated train control. No drivers.
All logic coded. All systems tested.
The Incident:
One winter night, thelast train of the daywas supposed
to stop atStation X— a suburban residential area.
Passengers waited on the platform.
But the traindidn’t stop.
This happenedagain the next night. And again.
Complaints poured in.
Engineers checked thetrain software— it was working.
Thestation systems— allﬁne.
No errors in the logs.
Finally, a system behavior specialist stepped in to
analyzenot just the parts, but the whole.
The Root Cause: Emergent Behavior
Here’s what they discovered:
•TheAI scheduling modulewas designed tooptimize energy usage.
•At night, whenfew passengers were detectedon the platform (based on infrared
sensors), and if the train wasbehind schedule, the AIskipped the stationto
conserve power and maintain timetable.
•The systemwas not malfunctioning— it wasbehaving exactly as designed.
•But it was doing somethingno human intended— leaving behind actual waiting
passengers in the dark.
Thesensors failed to detectpassengers wearing thick winter coats, standing still in the
cold — making them appear invisible to the thermal system.

--- PAGE 9 ---
Takeaway:
The system didn’t crash. It behaved — but the behavior wasemergent, based on subtle
interactions between sensors, algorithms, and assumptions.
Systems Engineering Insight:
This is whybehavior modelingandscenario-based analysisare critical.
Y ou must ask:
•What does the system do over time, under varying conditions?
•What if inputs are wrong, or incomplete?
•What feedback loops exist, and are they trustable?
Moral:
“Even when every part works, the system may not behave.”

Concept of ‘Components’ of the Systems 
Up to now, we have established a conceptual foundation for
understanding how systems act and are perceived — Function, State,
Situation, Behavior, and Process — all of which describewhat the
system is forandhow it operates.
But before we can meaningfully talk aboutstrategyor analyse a

What the system is made of — its Components — and how those

1.Bridges to the 'System' as a Whole

3.Foundation for Trade-off Analysis
◦Without knowing the components, we can’t evaluateredundancy, failure points,

4.Natural Progression for our Lecture Flow
◦We’ve covereddynamic/systemic concepts(state, process, etc.).
◦Next step:structural concepts(components, interfaces, interconnections).
◦Then we can unify both into astrategic system analysis.
Key Points to Cover in Components Discussion
•Deﬁnition: A component is a physical or logical part of a system, with

•Types: Hardware, software, human, environmental, informational.
•Interfaces: How components exchange material, energy, or information.
•Hierarchy: Components→Subsystems→System.
•Coupling & Cohesion: Tight vs. loose coupling, functional cohesion.
•Failure Modes: How component-level failure affects the whole system.

Set of components meets the following requirements*:
1.The properties and behavior of each component of the set have an effect on the properties
and behavior of the set as a whole.
2.The properties and behavior of each component of the set depend on the properties and
behavior of at least one other component in the set.
3.Each possible subset of components meets the two requirements listed above; the
components cannot be divided into independent subsets.
- Components of a system may themselves be systems, and every system may be part of
a larger system in a hierarchy
* requirements ensure that the set of components constituting a system always has some
property, or behavior pattern, that cannot be exhibited by any of its subsets acting alone.

What is Strategy in Systems Engineering?
In Systems Engineering,Strategyis theplanned set of decisions and guiding principles
that determinehow the system achieves its missionunder varying conditions.
It answers questions like:
•How will the system handle uncertainty?
•What will be done when key components fail?
•How will it scale when demand surges?
•How will it adapt when its operating environment changes?

2.Scenario-Based Thinking— Considersnormal,degraded, andfailure

Methodology for Identifying Functions in a System (Systems Engineering)
1.Clarify Stakeholders & Needs
◦Ask:“Who uses or depends on the system? What do they expect it to deliver?”
◦E.g., farmer→“I want consistent crop health with minimal water waste.”
2.Deﬁne System Boundary & Environment
◦What’s inside (controllable) vs. outside (external)?
◦E.g., sensors, pumps, controller (inside); weather (outside).
3.Abstract Purpose from Processes
◦Don’t confuse steps with function.
◦E.g., “Measure soil moisture” is a process, not a function.
◦Function is:Maintain optimal soil moisture.
1.Express in Input→Output terms
◦Identify what the systemtakes inand what itdelivers.
◦E.g., Input = soil condition, Output = water delivered to maintain target.
2.Generalize Beyond Implementation
◦Function should be solution-neutral.
◦E.g., Function = “Provide irrigation control” (not “Run pump motor”).
3.Check Measurability
◦Link function to KPIs (how do we know the function is fulﬁlled?).
◦E.g., soil moisture kept within band 80% of time.
4.Validate with Scenarios
◦V erify: Does this function hold in normal + stressed + degraded conditions?

Chapter Summary: From State to Function in Systems Engineering
1. State –“What the system is at a given
moment.”
•Deﬁnition:Internal condition snapshot of a
system or subsystem.
•Example:Smart Irrigation→Pump ON,
moisture = 18%.
•Why it matters:States deﬁne the “now”→
form the basis for analysis.
2. Situation –“State in context.”
•Deﬁnition:State + environment +
external factors (time, place, constraints).
•Example:Pump ON + Moisture 18% +
Reservoir low + Summer heat.
•Why it matters:Situations tell us
whether a state iscritical, normal, or
risky.
3. Behavior –“How the system responds
dynamically.”
•Deﬁnition:Observable response to
triggers, captured astransitions
between states.
•Example:If rain detected→Pump
shuts off in 2 minutes.
•Why it matters:Behaviors reveal
adaptability, stability, or failure
patterns.
4. Process –“Behaviors organized over
time.”
•Deﬁnition:Sequence + timing + logic of
behaviors that achieve outcomes.
•Example (Irrigation):
Measure→Forecast→Plan→Actuate→
V erify→Stop.
•Why it matters:Processes let us design
workﬂows, verify correctness, and plan
resilience paths.
5. Function –“Why the system exists at all.”
•Deﬁnition:The ultimate purpose of the system, expressed in terms of value
delivered.
•Example:Maintain crop soil health efﬁciently.
•Why it matters:Functions provide theNorth Star— everything else (states,
situations, behaviors, processes) serves the function.
Connecting the Concepts
•Stateis the building block.
•Situationaddscontext.
•Behaviorshowsdynamic change.
•Processorganizes behavior intostructured operation.
•Functionanchors it all withpurpose.
