Comparative Analysis of CPU Scheduling Algorithms for
Performance Efficiency
Sagili Sreenivas Reddy, VIT University, Vellore, India

Abstract—Scheduling is a key function of the
operating system because all computer resources need to
be allocated before they can be used. Among these
resources, the CPU stands out as particularly crucial,
and how it's scheduled can significantly affect the
overall performance of the system. When multiple
processes are ready to run at the same time, the
operating system depends on the scheduler to figure out
the order of execution. The method it uses to make this
decision is known as the scheduling algorithm. There
are many CPU scheduling algorithms out there, each
with its own unique performance traits depending on the
workload. This study takes a closer look at several
popular algorithms, evaluating their efficiency based on
important performance metrics like waiting time and
turnaround time. We set up a simulation framework to
calculate and compare these metrics across different
algorithms using representative sample datasets. The
findings reveal notable differences in how each
algorithm behaves, showing that the choice of
scheduling strategy can directly influence system
responsiveness and throughput. By carefully examining
these methods, the study sheds light on the strengths and
weaknesses of each algorithm, helping us better
understand how they can be applied in various
operating system environments.

I. INTRODUCTION
A scheduler is a key component of an operating system
(OS) that decides which job gets to enter the system next
and which process should run. Typically, operating
systems have three types of schedulers: the long-term
scheduler (also known as the job scheduler), the mediumterm scheduler, and the short-term scheduler, which is
often called the CPU scheduler or dispatcher. Each of
these schedulers has a unique role in managing system
load, optimizing resource use, and enhancing overall
responsiveness, making them essential for effective
operating system design.
CPU scheduling is crucial in multiprogrammed
operating systems, where the goal is to keep the CPU
busy as much as possible. In a uniprocessor setup, only
one process can run at a time, meaning all other processes
have to wait in the ready queue until the CPU is free. By
thoughtfully determining the order in which processes are
executed, CPU scheduling not only boosts utilization but
also impacts key performance indicators like throughput,
turnaround time, waiting time, and response time.
Scheduling, in a broader sense, is all about assigning
computational tasks to the resources that are available for
execution. These tasks can include processes, threads, or

data flows, while the resources might be processors,
network links, or I/O subsystems. The effectiveness of a
scheduling strategy has a direct impact on system
performance, especially in situations where the demand
for resources outstrips what’s immediately available.
That’s where scheduling algorithms come into play,
helping operating systems maintain fairness, boost
performance, and ensure that the system behaves
predictably.
Over the years, a variety of CPU scheduling algorithms
have emerged, each tailored to different workload
characteristics and performance goals. Classic algorithms
like First-Come First-Served (FCFS), Shortest Job Next
(SJN), Round Robin (RR), and Priority Scheduling are
still among the most studied. Meanwhile, more advanced
techniques aim to find a sweet spot between fairness and
efficiency by adapting dynamically to changes in
workload. It’s essential to evaluate these algorithms under
various conditions to figure out how well they perform in
real-world applications, especially as modern systems
increasingly require both high throughput and low
latency.

II. BACKGROUND
A. Types of Schedulers
Schedulers are a key part of any operating system,
playing a crucial role in deciding how jobs and processes
get their turn with the CPU and other resources. There are
three main types of schedulers that operating systems
typically use: long-term, medium-term, and short-term
schedulers. Each one has its own unique function that
helps keep the system running smoothly and efficiently by
handling the system performance and resource
management [1].
Long-term Scheduler: Often called the admission
scheduler, the long-term scheduler is responsible for
determining which jobs or processes get to enter the ready
queue in the main memory. When a program is submitted
for execution, it’s up to the long-term scheduler to either
approve or delay its entry into the active processes. This
decision is vital as it influences how many processes can
run simultaneously, ensuring a good mix of input/outputheavy and CPU-heavy tasks to keep everything running at
optimal speed[2].
Medium-term Scheduler: The medium-term
scheduler, sometimes referred to as the mid-term
scheduler, has the job of temporarily removing (or
swapping out) processes that aren’t currently active, have
lower priority, or are using too much memory. By doing
this, it clears up memory for other processes, boosting
overall system efficiency. When there’s enough memory

available again or when a process is ready to run, the
swapped-out process can be brought back in (swapped in)
[3].
Short-term Scheduler: Known as the CPU scheduler,
the short-term scheduler picks from the processes in
memory that are ready to run and assigns the CPU to one
of them. Unlike the long-term and medium-term
schedulers, this one makes decisions much more
frequently, often every few milliseconds. Short-term
scheduling can be preemptive, meaning it can forcibly
take a process off the CPU, or non-preemptive, where a
process keeps control of the CPU until it finishes or
voluntarily gives it up[4].
B. CPU Scheduling Algorithms
CPU scheduling is all about figuring out the order in
which processes in the ready queue get their turn with the
CPU. There are a bunch of different algorithms out there,
each designed with specific goals in mind, like fairness,
turnaround time, throughput, and how responsive the
system is. Let’s take a look at some of the most important
ones [5].
First Come First Serve (FCFS): This is the most
straightforward scheduling algorithm. It simply gives the
CPU to the process that shows up first in the ready queue.
Think of it like a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) line. It’s nonpreemptive, which means once a process gets the CPU, it
keeps going until it finishes or needs to do some I/O.
While it’s easy to understand, FCFS can create a “convoy
effect,” where shorter processes end up waiting behind
longer ones, making it less ideal for time-sharing systems
[6].
Shortest Job First (SJF): Also known as Shortest Job
Next (SJN) or Shortest Process Next (SPN), this nonpreemptive algorithm picks the process that’s estimated to
take the least time to execute. SJF is great for minimizing
average waiting time, making it theoretically the best
option under certain conditions. However, it has a couple
of significant downsides: long processes can get starved if
shorter ones keep coming in, and it requires knowing how
long each process will take, which isn’t always realistic in
the real world[7].
Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF): This is the
preemptive version of SJF. The SRTF algorithm chooses
the process with the least remaining burst time. If a new
process arrives that has a shorter remaining burst time
than the one currently running, the CPU gets switched
over. This makes the system more responsive but can lead
to increased overhead because of all the context switching
[8].
Round Robin (RR): Round Robin is a popular choice in
time-sharing systems. Each process gets a set amount of
time, known as a time quantum. If it doesn't finish within
that time, it's paused and moved to the back of the line.
The CPU then hands over control to the next process
waiting in the queue. This method promotes fairness and
helps prevent starvation, but its effectiveness really hinges
on how the time quantum is chosen. A smaller quantum

can lead to more context-switching overhead, while a
larger one might make the system less responsive [9].
Priority Scheduling: In this method, every process is
given a priority level, and the CPU is assigned to the one
with the highest priority. If multiple processes have the
same priority, the scheduling follows a First-Come, FirstServed (FCFS) approach. Priority scheduling can be
either preemptive or non-preemptive. However, a
significant downside is that low-priority processes might
starve, although this can be alleviated by using aging
techniques [10].
Future Knowledge Scheduling (FKS): This strategy
keeps the CPU idle until a process with the shortest burst
time arrives. Once that job comes in, it gets executed first,
and the rest follow in a Shortest Job First order. While
FKS does a great job of minimizing average waiting time,
its dependence on knowing future burst times makes it
impractical for real-world systems, limiting its use mostly
to theoretical discussions [11].
Highest Response Ratio Next (HRRN): HRRN is a
non-preemptive algorithm that takes the best of Shortest
Job First (SJF) while avoiding starvation [12]. It
calculates the priority of each process dynamically using
this formula:
Response Ratio = 1 + Waiting Time/Burst Time
Beyond the traditional methods, researchers have been
diving into hybrid scheduling algorithms that mix the best
features of various approaches. For instance, they might
combine priority scheduling with Round Robin to strike a
balance between fairness and responsiveness [13].
Studies have also looked into how the choice of
scheduler affects system performance metrics, revealing
that the best algorithm really depends on the workload—
there's no one-size-fits-all solution [14].
Moreover, real-time operating systems frequently
utilize specialized scheduling algorithms like Rate
Monotonic Scheduling (RMS) and Earliest Deadline First
(EDF) to meet strict timing requirements, highlighting
that scheduling is often tailored to specific applications
[15].
Lastly, the rise of modern multiprocessor and multicore systems has added new layers of complexity to
scheduling, necessitating load balancing and affinitybased algorithms to ensure fairness and efficiency across
multiple CPUs [16]. All in all, CPU scheduling is a
crucial function of operating systems, laying the
groundwork for optimizing performance and ensuring fair
resource allocation [17].
2.3 Key Concepts in CPU Scheduling
CPU Utilization: This term refers to how much time the
CPU is actively working on executing processes. The
main aim of CPU scheduling is to keep this percentage as
high as possible, ensuring the CPU is busy and
minimizing any idle time.

Throughput: Throughput is all about the number of
processes that finish execution in a given time frame. A
higher throughput means the scheduler is doing a great
job of balancing the load and cutting down on waiting
times in the system.
Burst Time: Burst time is the total CPU time a process
needs to complete its execution. Scheduling algorithms
often use burst time as a key factor in determining the
order of execution, especially in methods like Shortest
Job First and similar approaches.

Round Robin promotes fairness by giving each
process an equal slice of CPU time (known as

effectiveness really hinges on the size of the
time quantum. If it’s too large, RR starts to act
like FCFS; if it’s too small, you end up with too
much context switching, which can hurt CPU
efficiency.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Scheduling
Algorithms:
A. First Come First Serve (FCFS)
The FCFS algorithm is pretty straightforward
and easy to use since it schedules processes
based on their arrival order. This simplicity
means that every process eventually gets its turn
with the CPU, so there's no risk of starvation.
However, because it doesn’t allow for
preemption, FCFS isn’t the best choice for
interactive systems. If a long process takes over,
it can create a “convoy effect,” where shorter
processes end up waiting far too long, which can
really hurt system responsiveness.

D. Priority Scheduling
Priority scheduling lets you assign different
importance levels to processes based on factors
like memory, CPU usage, or user-defined
criteria. This is especially beneficial for highpriority tasks, such as real-time applications.
However, lower-priority processes might end up
starving if higher-priority jobs keep hogging the
CPU. To address this issue, it’s often necessary
to implement a secondary scheduling policy or
an aging mechanism to help balance things out.
E. Highest Response Ratio Next (HRRN)
HRRN strikes a balance between efficiency and
fairness by determining process priority based
on both how long they've been waiting and their
burst time. This approach effectively avoids
starvation, as processes that wait longer get
bumped up in priority. While it generally offers
stable average performance across various
workloads, it might not always deliver the best
results for specific scenarios, making it a bit less
predictable compared to deterministic
algorithms.
F. Future Knowledge Scheduling (FKS)
FKS showcases theoretical efficiency by
reducing waiting time when burst times are
known ahead of time. It stops larger processes
from hogging the CPU by giving priority to
shorter jobs, regardless of when they arrive.
However, this method isn't very practical in realworld situations since we can't accurately predict
future burst times. Plus, the CPU might end up
sitting idle while waiting for the shortest job to
show up, which can lower overall system
efficiency.

When the incoming processes are short and don’t need
to follow a particular order, First-Come, First-Served
(FCFS) is the way to go. It outperforms Shortest Job First
(SJF) and Round Robin (RR) in this case because the

each process being handled one after the other, every task
gets its turn.
In cases where you have a mix of long and short
processes, and everything needs to be completed within a
certain timeframe, Round Robin scheduling really shines.
It prevents any process from being starved of resources
and ensures that each one gets a fair share of time to
execute.
When dealing with a combination of user-based and
kernel-based processes, priority-based scheduling is the
most effective approach. Typically, kernel-based
processes are given higher priority than user-based ones.
For instance, the scheduler itself is a kernel-based
process, so it needs to run first to manage the scheduling
of other processes effectively.

VI. CONCLUSION
The comparative analysis of fundamental CPU
scheduling algorithms shows that how well a
scheduling method works really hinges on the specific
workload characteristics and system needs. From the
simulation results, it’s clear that Shortest Remaining
Time First (SRTF) achieves the lowest average
turnaround and waiting times, while also boosting
throughput and CPU utilization for the dataset in
question. However, its dependence on accurate burst
time estimates and its varying performance across
different workloads can limit its overall effectiveness.
First Come First Serve (FCFS) is a straightforward and
dependable strategy, especially in batch processing
settings, but it can lead to longer waiting times when
lengthy processes take over. Round Robin (RR) shines
in situations where fairness is key, making sure that no
process gets left behind, although its effectiveness can
be influenced by the choice of time quantum. Priority-

In the end, there’s no one-size-fits-all scheduling
algorithm. Each one has its own strengths depending on
the circumstances: FCFS is great for simple sequential
tasks, RR promotes fairness in time-sharing systems,
SJF and SRTF are best for minimizing turnaround time
with predictable workloads, HRRN strikes a balance
between efficiency and fairness, and priority
scheduling works well in systems that need to handle
processes differently. So, the choice of scheduling
policy should really be based on the operating
environment, the mix of processes, and the
performance objectives.
